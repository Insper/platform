System Architecture — 2027.1

Most systems you will build or maintain in your career have to be fast, available, consistent enough, secure and affordable — at the same time, under load, while parts of them fail. This edition of the course is about how to design such systems, how to prove with numbers that a design works, and how to explain it — to a team in a design review, and to an interviewer in a system-design interview.

What changes in 2027.1

The course is reorganized around the building blocks of modern architectures — the same palette of components you will find in the System Design Playground: client, traffic & edge, compute, storage, messaging, observability, network, AI & agents and external services.

  • Every class teaches a group of components, with the trade-offs, the numbers and the failure modes that matter in practice and in interviews.
  • Every class page embeds the playground, preloaded with a scenario, so you can see the concept under load: change a parameter, run a load test with randomness (crashes, spikes, hot keys, region outages), read the report.
  • The hands-on labs build one system along the semester — BoxOffice, a ticketing platform with a flash sale at 10:00 — and every lab ends with a "Measure it" step.
  • The Support section has the interview material, a workbook, a cheat sheet, a concept map and study cards.

The running example: BoxOffice

A stadium show goes on sale at 10:00. Two million fans, sixty thousand seats. The catalog is read-heavy, the seat inventory must never sell the same seat twice, payments go through an external provider that sometimes slows down, confirmations go out by e-mail, bots try to buy everything, and an AI assistant answers "find me two seats under R$ 300 for Saturday and hold them". Almost every topic of the course appears in this one system.

The story of the course

The classes are not a list of topics: they are the chapters of one story. BoxOffice starts as one service and one database, and every chapter begins with the problem the previous one left, stated with a number. A component enters the design only when a number asks for it, and each one brings a new problem. Press play to follow the chain:

Each row of the table is one chapter: the problem that opens it, the class that answers it, the lab where you build the answer in BoxOffice (and the file to read first), and the playground scenario where you can see it fail and recover. The planning puts the chapters on the calendar.

The problem (BoxOffice) Class Built in Code to read See it
1 Nobody has written down what "works" means: how many fans, how fast, how available. What is System Design Lab 0 scripts/check-env.sh monolith, flash-sale
2 Can one PostgreSQL take the 10:00 on-sale? Demand is ~33,000 holds/s; one database does ~1,100 at a sane utilization. Estimation Lab 1, Lab 2 load/stress.js, scripts/sweep.sh monolith (breakpoint)
3 A 30× gap means many machines, and between machines the network loses, delays and duplicates messages: a retried "buy" can charge twice. Distributed Systems Lab 2 (the knee, measured) — db-replicas, multi-region
4 Two million phones press refresh at 10:00:00. Most of those requests must never reach a service, the rest must reach a healthy replica, and every caller must prove who it is. Traffic & Edge Lab 3, Lab 4 infra/nginx/nginx.conf, TokenService.java scale-out, cdn-static, rate-limiter
5 Behind the edge, replicas come and go: a replica that keeps the session or the hold in memory loses it. What must a service be to scale out? Compute & Services Lab 4 SecurityConfig.java (gateway) microservices, serverless-burst
6 Stateless services push every decision into the database, and 2 fans click the same seat in the same millisecond. Databases Lab 6 HoldService.java db-replicas, sharding, db-failover
7 The event pages are read ~100× more than they change; the database spends its capacity answering the same question. Caching Lab 5 EventCache.java read-heavy-cache
8 Checkout waits for the e-mail and the provider; when the e-mail server is down, nobody can buy. 30× more fans than capacity need a fair line. Messaging Lab 7 Outbox.java, WaitingRoom.java async-queue, event-fanout
9 Asynchrony hides failures: an order vanishes between Kafka and the e-mail, and the dashboards stay green while fans cannot pay. What exactly do we promise (the SLO), and how do we know we keep it? Observability Lab 8 slo-catalog.yaml observability-stack
10 Now we can see it: at 10:02 the payment provider answers in 4 s instead of 200 ms. Nothing is down, yet every order thread waits on it and checkout dies with it. Reliability Lab 9 PaymentClient.java cascading-failure, retry-storm, db-failover
11 Compose on one machine does not restart a dead replica on another node, scale at 09:45 or roll a bad release back. Cloud-Native Lab 10, Lab 11 catalog-hpa.yaml, catalog-rollout.yaml k8s-node-failure, serverless-burst
12 Many services now share one platform: bots buy the best seats, a token leaks, every service trusts every other one, and nobody checks what the pipeline ships. Network & Security Lab 11 (supply chain) .github/dependabot.yml payment-system, flash-sale
13 Fans ask "two seats under R$ 300 on Saturday?": the answer comes from a slow, expensive, non-deterministic dependency that reads the catalog and the seat map through tools, and must never invent a seat or obey text hidden in a page. AI Systems Lab 12 agent.py, tools.py ai-assistant
14 With AI writing code, changes arrive faster than people can review, test and release them. AI-Assisted Delivery Lab 13 review.py ai-assistant
15 Every chapter added a box. Does the whole design hold the next on-sale, and what does it cost? Performance Engineering Lab 11, Project load/budget.js flash-sale
16 A different system tomorrow (a feed, a chat, a ride app): the same blocks, other numbers. Case Studies Exercises — one per case

How to read a class with the story in mind

Every class opens with "The story so far" (where BoxOffice is and which number forces this class) and ends with "What breaks next" (the limit of what you just learned, which opens the next chapter). Every subpage has a "How it connects" map, a "Best practices" box (the habits a reviewer expects, next to the pitfalls to avoid) and, when a lab implements the idea, an "In the BoxOffice code" box with the real file; every lab lists the best practices its code applies. When you can say, for each component, which number put it there and what it costs, you can defend the design — in a review or in an interview.

Grade

\[ \text{Final Grade} = \left\{\begin{array}{lll} \text{Individual} \geq 5 \bigwedge \text{Team} \geq 5 & \implies & \displaystyle \frac{ \text{Individual} + \text{Team} } {2} \\ \\ \text{Otherwise} & \implies & \min\left(\text{Individual}, \text{Team}\right) \end{array}\right. \]

  1. Exercises (individual): seven exercises — estimation, load-test report, cache & database, event-driven, Kubernetes autoscaling, playground design and design doc — each graded with a concept (A–I) and a rubric. Weights and rules are on the exercises page.

  2. Quizzes: three quizzes; the lowest is dropped and the grade is the average of the two best:

    \[ \text{Quizzes} = \frac{\text{Q}_1 + \text{Q}_2 + \text{Q}_3 - \min(\text{Q}_1, \text{Q}_2, \text{Q}_3)}{2} \]

  3. Exams: a midterm and a final exam, individual, covering the classes and the labs. Part of each exam is a short system-design question in the format of the interview framework.

  4. Team project: teams of 2 or 3 students build, measure, break on purpose and defend one system, in nine milestones (design doc, core path, scale & cache, async & resilience, Kubernetes + CI/CD, load-test campaign, chaos game day, cost report, presentation). The team grade is the same for all members. Details on the project page.

ORAL EXAMINATION

ALL GRADES in this course are subject to an oral examination for validation. If the outcome of the oral examination is negative, that specific grade will be ZEROED.

Use of AI tools

AI tools are allowed and are part of the course (classes 13 and 14 and lab 13 are about building with them). You are responsible for every line and every number you deliver; measurements must come from runs you did. The rules are on the exercises page.
